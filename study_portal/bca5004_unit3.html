💡 Conceptual Intuition: Why Do We Need Numerical Integration?
In analytical calculus, we learn integration tricks (substitution, integration by parts, partial fractions). But many real-world functions have no elementary anti-derivative: $$\int e^{-x^2}\,dx \quad \text{or} \quad \int \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx \quad \text{or} \quad \int \sqrt{1 + x^4}\,dx$$ Furthermore, in engineering experiments, we often don't have a mathematical function at all—we only have a table of discrete sensor readings (e.g. velocity over time)!
**Numerical Integration (Quadrature)** solves this by calculating the **definite area under the curve** by slicing the area into simple geometric strips (trapezoids or parabolas) whose areas can be added with basic arithmetic!
1. Numerical Differentiation Formulas
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2024–25, 2022–23 (Derive first and second derivatives from forward difference formula)

Numerical differentiation is the process of computing the value of the derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$ of a function at a given point using a discrete table of values.

Derivation from Newton's Forward Difference Formula:
Newton's forward formula states: $y(x) = y_0 + p \Delta y_0 + \frac{p(p-1)}{2} \Delta^2 y_0 + \frac{p(p-1)(p-2)}{6} \Delta^3 y_0 + \dots$ where $p = \frac{x - x_0}{h} \implies \frac{dp}{dx} = \frac{1}{h}$.
By the chain rule of differentiation: $$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{dp} \cdot \frac{dp}{dx} = \frac{1}{h} \left[\frac{d}{dp} y(x)\right]$$ Differentiating term-by-term with respect to $p$: $$\mathbf{\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)_{x = x_0} = \frac{1}{h}\left[\Delta y_0 - \frac{1}{2}\Delta^2 y_0 + \frac{1}{3}\Delta^3 y_0 - \frac{1}{4}\Delta^4 y_0 + \dots\right]}$$ Differentiating a second time yields the second derivative: $$\mathbf{\left(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right)_{x = x_0} = \frac{1}{h^2}\left[\Delta^2 y_0 - \Delta^3 y_0 + \frac{11}{12}\Delta^4 y_0 - \frac{5}{6}\Delta^5 y_0 + \dots\right]}$$
2. The General Quadrature Formula (Master Derivation)
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2023–24, 2021, 2019 (Fundamental 10-mark derivation from which all rules stem!)

Let $I = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. Divide the interval $[a, b]$ into $n$ equal subintervals of width $h = \frac{b - a}{n}$ with abscissas $x_0 = a, x_1 = x_0 + h, \dots, x_n = x_0 + nh = b$.

Step-by-Step Derivation:
Substitute $x = x_0 + uh \implies dx = h\,du$. When $x = x_0, u = 0$; when $x = x_n, u = n$.
Replacing $f(x)$ with Newton's forward difference interpolating polynomial: $$I = \int_{x_0}^{x_0 + nh} f(x)\,dx = \int_0^n \left[y_0 + u \Delta y_0 + \frac{u(u-1)}{2!} \Delta^2 y_0 + \frac{u(u-1)(u-2)}{3!} \Delta^3 y_0 + \dots\right] h\,du$$ Evaluating the definite integrals with respect to $u$: $$\int_0^n du = n, \quad \int_0^n u\,du = \frac{n^2}{2}, \quad \int_0^n \frac{u^2-u}{2}\,du = \frac{n^3}{6} - \frac{n^2}{4} = \frac{n^2(2n-3)}{12}$$ Factoring out $nh$, we obtain the **General Quadrature Formula**: $$\mathbf{\int_{x_0}^{x_0+nh} f(x)\,dx = nh\left[y_0 + \frac{n}{2}\Delta y_0 + \frac{n(2n-3)}{12}\Delta^2 y_0 + \frac{n(n-2)^2}{24}\Delta^3 y_0 + \dots\right]}$$
3. Deductions from General Quadrature
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2023–24, 2021 (State conditions and formulas for Trapezoidal, Simpson 1/3 and 3/8)

1. Trapezoidal Rule (Put $n = 1$)

Putting $n = 1$ in the General Quadrature formula and neglecting 2nd and higher differences ($\Delta^2 y_0 \approx 0$):

$$\int_{x_0}^{x_1} f(x)\,dx = h\left[y_0 + \frac{1}{2}\Delta y_0\right] = h\left[y_0 + \frac{1}{2}(y_1 - y_0)\right] = \frac{h}{2}(y_0 + y_1)$$

Summing across all $n$ subintervals gives the **Composite Trapezoidal Rule**:

$$\mathbf{\int_{x_0}^{x_n} f(x)\,dx = \frac{h}{2}\left[(y_0 + y_n) + 2(y_1 + y_2 + y_3 + \dots + y_{n-1})\right]}$$ Rule in Words: $\frac{h}{2} \left[(\text{Sum of First and Last ordinates}) + 2(\text{Sum of all remaining intermediate ordinates})\right]$.
• Constraint: $n$ can be ANY positive integer ($n \ge 1$).
• Geometric shape: Slices area into straight trapezoids.
• Truncation Error: $E_T = -\frac{(b-a)h^2}{12} f''(\xi)$. Degree of precision = 1.

2. Simpson's 1/3rd Rule (Put $n = 2$)

Putting $n = 2$ in the General Quadrature formula and neglecting 3rd and higher differences ($\Delta^3 y_0 \approx 0$):

$$\int_{x_0}^{x_2} f(x)\,dx = 2h\left[y_0 + \frac{2}{2}\Delta y_0 + \frac{2(4-3)}{12}\Delta^2 y_0\right] = 2h\left[y_0 + (y_1 - y_0) + \frac{1}{6}(y_2 - 2y_1 + y_0)\right] = \frac{h}{3}(y_0 + 4y_1 + y_2)$$

Summing across all pairs of subintervals gives the **Composite Simpson's 1/3rd Rule**:

$$\mathbf{\int_{x_0}^{x_n} f(x)\,dx = \frac{h}{3}\left[(y_0 + y_n) + 4(y_1 + y_3 + y_5 + \dots) + 2(y_2 + y_4 + y_6 + \dots)\right]}$$ Rule in Words: $\frac{h}{3} \left[(\text{First} + \text{Last}) + 4(\text{Sum of ODD ordinates}) + 2(\text{Sum of EVEN ordinates})\right]$.
• MANDATORY CONSTRAINT: The number of subintervals $n$ MUST BE AN **EVEN NUMBER** ($n = 2, 4, 6, 8, \dots$).
• Geometric shape: Replaces the curve with second-degree parabolas through sets of 3 points.
• Truncation Error: $E_{S1/3} = -\frac{(b-a)h^4}{180} f^{(4)}(\xi)$. Degree of precision = 3! (Exact for polynomials up to degree 3).

3. Simpson's 3/8th Rule (Put $n = 3$)

Putting $n = 3$ in the General Quadrature formula and neglecting 4th and higher differences:

$$\mathbf{\int_{x_0}^{x_n} f(x)\,dx = \frac{3h}{8}\left[(y_0 + y_n) + 3(y_1 + y_2 + y_4 + y_5 + \dots) + 2(y_3 + y_6 + y_9 + \dots)\right]}$$ Rule in Words: $\frac{3h}{8} \left[(\text{First} + \text{Last}) + 3(\text{Sum of non-multiples of 3}) + 2(\text{Sum of multiples of 3})\right]$.
• MANDATORY CONSTRAINT: The number of subintervals $n$ MUST BE A **MULTIPLE OF 3** ($n = 3, 6, 9, 12, \dots$).
• Geometric shape: Fits third-degree cubic polynomials through sets of 4 points.
• Truncation Error: $E_{S3/8} = -\frac{(b-a)h^4}{80} f^{(4)}(\xi)$.
4. Master Solved University Numerical Problem
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2023–24, 2021 (The classic $\int_0^6 \frac{dx}{1+x^2}$ problem!)

Problem: Evaluate $I = \int_0^6 \frac{dx}{1 + x^2}$ by dividing the range $[0, 6]$ into $n = 6$ equal subintervals using:
(i) Trapezoidal Rule
(ii) Simpson's 1/3rd Rule
(iii) Simpson's 3/8th Rule
Compare your answers with the exact analytical value.

Step 1: Compute Step Size $h$ and Table of Ordinates:

$$h = \frac{b - a}{n} = \frac{6 - 0}{6} = \mathbf{1}$$

Compute $y = f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ for $x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$:

Ordinate $x$ $y = f(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ Decimal Value (to 4 places)
$y_0$ (First)$0$$\frac{1}{1 + 0} = 1.0000$$1.0000$
$y_1$ (Odd)$1$$\frac{1}{1 + 1} = 0.5000$$0.5000$
$y_2$ (Even)$2$$\frac{1}{1 + 4} = 0.2000$$0.2000$
$y_3$ (Odd/Mult of 3)$3$$\frac{1}{1 + 9} = 0.1000$$0.1000$
$y_4$ (Even)$4$$\frac{1}{1 + 16} = 0.0588$$0.0588$
$y_5$ (Odd)$5$$\frac{1}{1 + 25} = 0.0385$$0.0385$
$y_6$ (Last)$6$$\frac{1}{1 + 36} = 0.0270$$0.0270$

Part (i): By Trapezoidal Rule ($h = 1$)

$$I_{Trap} = \frac{h}{2}\left[(y_0 + y_6) + 2(y_1 + y_2 + y_3 + y_4 + y_5)\right]$$ $$I_{Trap} = \frac{1}{2}\left[(1.0000 + 0.0270) + 2(0.5000 + 0.2000 + 0.1000 + 0.0588 + 0.0385)\right]$$ $$I_{Trap} = \frac{1}{2}\left[1.0270 + 2(0.8973)\right] = \frac{1}{2}[1.0270 + 1.7946] = \frac{2.8216}{2} = \mathbf{1.4108}$$

Part (ii): By Simpson's 1/3rd Rule ($n = 6$ is even!)

$$I_{S1/3} = \frac{h}{3}\left[(y_0 + y_6) + 4(y_1 + y_3 + y_5) + 2(y_2 + y_4)\right]$$ $$I_{S1/3} = \frac{1}{3}\left[(1.0000 + 0.0270) + 4(0.5000 + 0.1000 + 0.0385) + 2(0.2000 + 0.0588)\right]$$ $$I_{S1/3} = \frac{1}{3}\left[1.0270 + 4(0.6385) + 2(0.2588)\right] = \frac{1}{3}[1.0270 + 2.5540 + 0.5176] = \frac{4.0986}{3} = \mathbf{1.3662}$$

Part (iii): By Simpson's 3/8th Rule ($n = 6$ is a multiple of 3!)

$$I_{S3/8} = \frac{3h}{8}\left[(y_0 + y_6) + 3(y_1 + y_2 + y_4 + y_5) + 2(y_3)\right]$$ $$I_{S3/8} = \frac{3(1)}{8}\left[(1.0000 + 0.0270) + 3(0.5000 + 0.2000 + 0.0588 + 0.0385) + 2(0.1000)\right]$$ $$I_{S3/8} = \frac{3}{8}\left[1.0270 + 3(0.7973) + 0.2000\right] = \frac{3}{8}[1.0270 + 2.3919 + 0.2000] = \frac{3(3.6189)}{8} = \mathbf{1.3571}$$

Exact Analytical Comparison:

$$\int_0^6 \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = [\arctan(x)]_0^6 = \arctan(6) - \arctan(0) \approx \mathbf{1.4056}$$

Simpson's rules provide remarkable precision with only 6 discrete slices!

5. Exam Answer Blueprint: 10-Mark Model Structure
How to Score Full 10 Marks on "Derive Trapezoidal & Simpson's 1/3":
  1. General Quadrature (4 Marks): Integrate Newton's forward difference series $I = \int_0^n [\dots] h\,du$ to derive the master formula $nh[y_0 + \frac{n}{2}\Delta y_0 + \dots]$.
  2. Deduce Trapezoidal (2 Marks): Substitute $n = 1$, neglect $\Delta^2$, show single strip $\frac{h}{2}(y_0+y_1)$, then write composite formula.
  3. Deduce Simpson's 1/3 (3 Marks): Substitute $n = 2$, keep up to $\Delta^2$, show double strip $\frac{h}{3}(y_0+4y_1+y_2)$, then write composite formula. Emphasize that $n$ must be EVEN!
  4. Error Comparison (1 Mark): State that Trapezoidal error is $O(h^2)$ while Simpson's 1/3 is $O(h^4)$.
⚠️ Common Exam Pitfalls:
• Violating Parity Constraints: Applying Simpson's 1/3rd rule when $n$ is ODD (e.g. $n = 5$) is mathematically invalid and receives 0 marks! Simpson's 1/3rd strictly requires $n$ to be EVEN.
• Multipliers in Simpson's 1/3: Remember: 4 multiplies ODD ordinates ($y_1, y_3, y_5$) and 2 multiplies EVEN ordinates ($y_2, y_4, y_6$). Don't invert them!
