1. Executive Information Systems (EIS / ESS)
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2020, 2016 (Features, functionalities, difference from DSS, and system integration)

An Executive Information System (EIS), also termed an Executive Support System (ESS), is a specialized computer-based system tailored specifically for senior executive decision-making. Unlike operational systems dealing with detailed transactions, an EIS delivers a bird's-eye view of organizational health through high-level summaries, Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), trends, and exception alerts.

Key Architectural Characteristics of EIS:
  • Intuitive Graphical Interface: Touch/dashboard-friendly with minimal keyboard entry; uses graphs, traffic-light alerts, and heatmaps.
  • Drill-Down Capability: Executives can click on an aggregated figure (e.g., quarterly sales dip in Northern Zone) to drill down through regional branches to individual product invoices.
  • Internal + External Integration: Combines internal ERP/financial data with external competitive intelligence, stock indices, inflation rates, and regulatory news.
  • Exception Reporting & Trend Analysis: Automatically flags anomalies where actual metrics deviate beyond predefined thresholds (e.g., production delay > 15%).
System Operational Level Primary Role & Contribution to EIS
TPS (Transaction Processing) Operational (Daily) Supplies raw transactional data: sales entries, payroll, receipts, and order logs.

2. Business Expert Systems (BES) & AI in KM
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2023–24 (Meaning, components of an expert system, benefits, and KM goals)

Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Knowledge Management focuses on emulating human reasoning to capture tacit knowledge, discover hidden patterns, and automate decision paths. A Business Expert System (BES) is an AI application that solves complex, domain-specific problems by applying human expert heuristic rules.

Architecture of a Business Expert System
The 5 Core Components of an Expert System:
  1. Knowledge Base: Stores factual domain rules and heuristic rules expressed as IF-THEN clauses.
  2. Inference Engine: The brain of the system; uses Forward Chaining (data-driven) or Backward Chaining (goal-driven) to derive answers.
  3. User Interface: Dialog system translating non-expert queries into formal rule queries and returning advice.
  4. Explanation Facility (Justifier): Traces the exact fired rule chain to explain "Why" a question is asked and "How" the conclusion was reached.
  5. Knowledge Acquisition Subsystem: Module assisting knowledge engineers in encoding expert heuristics into rules.
3. OLTP vs OLAP (High-Yield Comparison)
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2023–24, 2016 (Frequently asked for 5–10 marks)

Modern data architectures split transactional duties from analytical duties to safeguard performance and system stability:

Feature OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) OLAP (Online Analytical Processing)
Primary Purpose Daily business operations & fast transaction execution Multi-dimensional data analysis & decision support
Nature of Data Current, highly detailed, real-time snapshot Historical, summarized, aggregated across years
Database Design Normalized (3NF/BCNF) to eliminate redundancy Denormalized (Star or Snowflake Schema) for fast reads
Operations Frequent INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE Mostly heavy SELECT, aggregation, and reads
Query Complexity Simple, standardized queries touching single rows Complex analytical queries spanning millions of rows
Users Clerks, bank tellers, front-desk operators Business analysts, data scientists, senior executives
Real Example Withdrawing money from an ATM; booking a train ticket Annual sales comparison of mobile phones across 5 states
4. Data Warehouse & 3-Tier Architecture
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2023–24, 2020, 2019 (Characteristics, ETL process, and 3-tier architecture diagram)

According to William H. Inmon (the father of data warehousing):
"A Data Warehouse is a Subject-Oriented, Integrated, Time-Variant, and Non-Volatile collection of data in support of management's decision-making process."

Classic 3-Tier Data Warehouse Architecture
The 4 Inmon Characteristics Decoded:
  • Subject-Oriented: Modeled around high-level entities (e.g. Sales, Customer, Product) rather than individual transaction processes.
  • Integrated: Data from diverse, inconsistent operational legacy databases is standardized (e.g. mapping 'M'/'F', 1/0, and 'Male'/'Female' to a uniform standard).
  • Time-Variant: Every record carries a timestamp or date key to allow multi-year historical trend tracking (5–10 years).
  • Non-Volatile: Data is read-only after loading; records are never updated in place, only appended or queried.
5. Data Marts, Schemas & Top-Down vs Bottom-Up Design
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2018, 2015, 2014 (Data Mart vs Warehouse & Design Approaches)

A Data Mart is a specialized, smaller subset of a data warehouse focused strictly on a single functional department (e.g., Marketing Data Mart, Payroll Data Mart).

Feature Top-Down Approach (Inmon) Bottom-Up Approach (Kimball)
Starting Point Builds centralized Enterprise Data Warehouse (EDW) first Builds individual departmental Data Marts first
Integration Method Built directly into central design Integrates marts later using Conformed Dimensions
Initial Time & Cost High initial cost; takes 1–3 years to deliver initial value Lower initial cost; delivers initial reports in weeks/months
Scalability & Consistency Highest enterprise-wide consistency; highly robust Fast initial ROI; high risk of data inconsistency if unmanaged

Star Schema vs Snowflake Schema:

Star Schema: Contains a central Fact Table surrounded by completely denormalized Dimension Tables. Requires fewer SQL joins, leading to blazing-fast OLAP queries.

Snowflake Schema: An extension where dimension tables are normalized into sub-tables (e.g. City → State → Country). Saves storage space and minimizes redundancy, but requires multi-table joins which can slow down large analytical queries.
