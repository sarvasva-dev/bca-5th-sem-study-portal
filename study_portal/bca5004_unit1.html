💡 Conceptual Intuition: Why Do We Need Numerical Methods to Find Roots?
In school, we solve quadratic equations $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ easily using the quadratic formula $x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$. But what if you encounter an equation like: $$x e^x - 1 = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x^3 - x - 11 = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \cos(x) - x = 0$$ By the Abel-Ruffini theorem, general polynomials of degree 5 or higher (and transcendental equations mixing algebra with trigonometry/exponentials) have **no closed-form algebraic formula**!
**Numerical Methods** solve this by starting with a rough guess and generating a sequence of approximations $x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots \to \alpha$ that zoom in on the true root with pinpoint decimal precision!
1. Mathematical Foundations & The Bracketing Principle
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2024–25, 2021 (Sign test for root existence, bracketing interval)

A real number $\alpha$ is defined as a root (or zero) of an equation $f(x) = 0$ if and only if $f(\alpha) = 0$. Geometrically, a real root is the $x$-coordinate where the graph $y = f(x)$ crosses or touches the $x$-axis.

The Bracketing Principle (Intermediate Value Theorem):
Let $f(x)$ be a continuous function on a closed interval $[a, b]$. If the function values at the endpoints have opposite algebraic signs: $$f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0$$ then there exists at least one real root $\alpha \in (a, b)$ such that $f(\alpha) = 0$.

Every bracketing algorithm (Bisection, Regula-Falsi) requires an initial sign-changing bracket $[a, b]$ before beginning iterations.

2. The Bisection Method & Convergence Proof
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2023–24, 2018 (Write steps, prove guaranteed convergence, solve $x^3-x-11=0$)

The Bisection Method (or Interval Halving Method) repeatedly divides a bracketing interval $[a, b]$ in half and selects the sub-interval that maintains opposite signs.

Algorithm (Standard 7-Step University Exam Answer):
  1. Choose initial endpoints $a$ and $b$ such that $f(a) \cdot f(b) < 0$.
  2. Calculate the interval midpoint: $c = \frac{a + b}{2}$.
  3. Evaluate $f(c)$. If $f(c) = 0$, then $c$ is the exact root; stop.
  4. If $f(a) \cdot f(c) < 0$, the root lies in $[a, c]$; set $b = c$.
  5. If $f(c) \cdot f(b) < 0$, the root lies in $[c, b]$; set $a = c$.
  6. Check stopping criteria: If $|b - a| < \epsilon$ or $|f(c)| < \delta$, stop.
  7. Otherwise, repeat Steps 2–6 until required decimal accuracy is reached.

Proof of Guaranteed Convergence (CSJMU 2018):

Let $[a_n, b_n]$ denote the bracket at the $n$-th iteration. At each step, the interval width is halved:

$$b_n - a_n = \frac{b - a}{2^n}$$

As $n \to \infty$, since $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{b - a}{2^n} = 0$, the interval width shrinks to zero.

The lower bounds form a bounded, monotonically non-decreasing sequence: $a_1 \le a_2 \le a_3 \le \dots \le b_1$.
The upper bounds form a bounded, monotonically non-increasing sequence: $b_1 \ge b_2 \ge b_3 \ge \dots \ge a_1$.
By the Bolzano-Weierstrass Monotone Convergence Theorem, both sequences converge to a common limit $\alpha$:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} b_n = \alpha$$

Since $f$ is continuous and $f(a_n) \cdot f(b_n) \le 0$ for all $n$, taking limits yields $f(\alpha) \cdot f(\alpha) \le 0 \implies [f(\alpha)]^2 \le 0$. Since a real square cannot be negative, $f(\alpha) = 0$. Hence, the Bisection Method is mathematically guaranteed to converge!

Error Bound and Iteration Count Formula:

The maximum error after $n$ iterations is bounded by: $E_n = |c_n - \alpha| \le \frac{b - a}{2^{n+1}}$. To guarantee error strictly less than tolerance $\epsilon$:

$$\frac{b - a}{2^{n+1}} < \epsilon \implies 2^{n+1} > \frac{b - a}{\epsilon} \implies n > \log_2\left(\frac{b - a}{\epsilon}\right) - 1 = \frac{\ln((b-a)/\epsilon)}{\ln 2} - 1$$

3. Regula-Falsi (Method of False Position)
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2020 (Formula derivation, geometric chord meaning, solved numericals)

The Regula-Falsi Method improves on Bisection by taking into account the actual magnitudes of $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. Instead of choosing the midpoint, it connects $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ by a straight chord and takes the $x$-intercept of that chord as the next approximation.

Algebraic Derivation of the Regula-Falsi Formula:
The two-point equation of the straight secant line passing through $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ is: $$\frac{y - f(a)}{x - a} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$ To find the $x$-intercept, set $y = 0$: $$\frac{-f(a)}{x - a} = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \implies x - a = -\frac{f(a)(b - a)}{f(b) - f(a)}$$ $$x = a - \frac{f(a)(b - a)}{f(b) - f(a)} = \frac{a[f(b) - f(a)] - f(a)(b - a)}{f(b) - f(a)} = \mathbf{\frac{a f(b) - b f(a)}{f(b) - f(a)}}$$

Step-by-Step Solved University Numerical

Problem: Find the positive root of $f(x) = x^2 - 5 = 0$ (i.e. $\sqrt{5}$) using Regula-Falsi on $[2, 3]$.

• $f(2) = 2^2 - 5 = -1 < 0$
• $f(3) = 3^2 - 5 = +4 > 0$
$f(2) \cdot f(3) < 0 \implies$ Bracket is $[2, 3]$ with $a = 2, f(a) = -1$ and $b = 3, f(b) = 4$.

Iteration 1:

$$x_1 = \frac{a f(b) - b f(a)}{f(b) - f(a)} = \frac{(2)(4) - (3)(-1)}{4 - (-1)} = \frac{8 + 3}{5} = \frac{11}{5} = \mathbf{2.2000}$$ $$f(2.2) = (2.2)^2 - 5 = 4.84 - 5 = -0.16 < 0$$ Since $f(2.2)$ is negative, replace $a$ with $2.2000$. New bracket is $[2.2000, 3.0000]$.

Iteration 2:

$$x_2 = \frac{(2.2)(4) - (3)(-0.16)}{4 - (-0.16)} = \frac{8.8 + 0.48}{4.16} = \frac{9.28}{4.16} \approx \mathbf{2.2308}$$ $$f(2.2308) = (2.2308)^2 - 5 = 4.9765 - 5 = -0.0235$$ Since $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.2360$, Regula-Falsi approaches the root much faster than Bisection in fewer steps!
4. Newton-Raphson Method & Quadratic Convergence Proof ($p=2$)
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2023–24, 2022–23, 2020, 2018 (Most heavily tested question in the syllabus!)

The Newton-Raphson Method is an open root-finding method that uses the tangent line to the function at the current point $x_n$ to estimate the next point $x_{n+1}$.

Derivation via Taylor's Theorem:
Let $x_n$ be an approximation to the root $\alpha$. Let $h = x_{n+1} - x_n$ be the correction. Expanding $f(x_n + h)$ by Taylor series: $$f(x_n + h) = f(x_n) + h f'(x_n) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x_n) + \dots = 0$$ Assuming $h$ is small, neglect second- and higher-order terms: $$f(x_n) + h f'(x_n) \approx 0 \implies h = -\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$$ Since $x_{n+1} = x_n + h$, we obtain the celebrated **Newton-Raphson Iterative Formula**: $$\mathbf{x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}}$$

Complete Proof of Quadratic Convergence ($p = 2$):

Let $\alpha$ be the true root such that $f(\alpha) = 0$ and $f'(\alpha) \ne 0$. Let $e_n = x_n - \alpha$ be the error at step $n$, so $x_n = \alpha + e_n$ and $x_{n+1} = \alpha + e_{n+1}$. Substituting into the formula:

$$\alpha + e_{n+1} = \alpha + e_n - \frac{f(\alpha + e_n)}{f'(\alpha + e_n)}$$ $$e_{n+1} = e_n - \frac{f(\alpha + e_n)}{f'(\alpha + e_n)}$$

Expanding $f(\alpha + e_n)$ and $f'(\alpha + e_n)$ using Taylor series about $\alpha$ (remembering $f(\alpha) = 0$):

$$f(\alpha + e_n) = e_n f'(\alpha) + \frac{e_n^2}{2} f''(\alpha) + O(e_n^3)$$ $$f'(\alpha + e_n) = f'(\alpha) + e_n f''(\alpha) + O(e_n^2) = f'(\alpha)\left[1 + e_n \frac{f''(\alpha)}{f'(\alpha)}\right]$$

Dividing numerator by denominator using binomial expansion $(1 + z)^{-1} \approx 1 - z$:

$$\frac{f(\alpha + e_n)}{f'(\alpha + e_n)} = \left[e_n + \frac{e_n^2}{2} \frac{f''(\alpha)}{f'(\alpha)}\right] \cdot \left[1 - e_n \frac{f''(\alpha)}{f'(\alpha)}\right] = e_n - \frac{e_n^2}{2}\frac{f''(\alpha)}{f'(\alpha)} + O(e_n^3)$$

Now substitute back into the error equation:

$$e_{n+1} = e_n - \left[e_n - \frac{e_n^2}{2}\frac{f''(\alpha)}{f'(\alpha)}\right] = \mathbf{\left[\frac{f''(\alpha)}{2 f'(\alpha)}\right] e_n^2}$$

Hence, $e_{n+1} \propto e_n^2$. The order of convergence is strictly **$p = 2$ (Quadratic)**! The number of correct decimal places doubles at each subsequent iteration!

The 3 Critical Failure Conditions of Newton-Raphson:

  1. Zero Derivative ($f'(x_n) = 0$): Tangent is horizontal and parallel to the $x$-axis; division by zero occurs and the method crashes.
  2. Inflection Point ($f''(x) = 0$ near root): The method can oscillate indefinitely in an infinite 2-cycle loop ($x_0 \to x_1 \to x_0 \to x_1$).
  3. Poor Initial Guess: If $x_0$ is chosen too far from $\alpha$, the tangent shoots off towards infinity, diverging away from the root.

Step-by-Step Solved University Numerical

Problem: Find $\sqrt{12}$ correct to 4 decimal places using Newton-Raphson.

Let $x = \sqrt{12} \implies x^2 - 12 = 0$. Hence $f(x) = x^2 - 12$ and $f'(x) = 2x$.

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{x_n^2 - 12}{2x_n} = \frac{2x_n^2 - x_n^2 + 12}{2x_n} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}\left(x_n + \frac{12}{x_n}\right)}$$

Take initial guess $x_0 = 3.5$ (since $3^2 = 9$ and $4^2 = 16$):

• Iteration 1: $x_1 = \frac{1}{2}\left(3.5 + \frac{12}{3.5}\right) = \frac{1}{2}(3.5 + 3.42857) = \mathbf{3.46429}$

• Iteration 2: $x_2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(3.46429 + \frac{12}{3.46429}\right) = \frac{1}{2}(3.46429 + 3.46391) = \mathbf{3.46410}$

Notice that in just 2 iterations, we have achieved $\sqrt{12} \approx \mathbf{3.4641}$ correct to 5 significant digits!

5. Descartes' Rule of Signs
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2024–25, 2021, 2018 (State Descartes rule and find bounds on roots of polynomial)

Descartes' Rule of Signs provides an upper bound on the number of positive and negative real roots of a polynomial equation with real coefficients without needing to solve it:

The Rule:
  • Positive Real Roots: The number of positive real roots of $P(x) = 0$ is either equal to the number of sign variations in the coefficients of $P(x)$, or less than it by an even integer.
  • Negative Real Roots: The number of negative real roots of $P(x) = 0$ is either equal to the number of sign variations in the coefficients of $P(-x)$, or less than it by an even integer.
  • Complex Roots: If a polynomial of degree $n$ has $p$ positive roots and $q$ negative roots, the remaining roots $n - (p + q)$ are complex conjugate pairs!

Solved Example:

Consider $P(x) = x^4 - 3x^3 + 7x^2 - 5x + 2 = 0$ (Degree $n = 4$):

1. Sign sequence of $P(x)$: $+1, -3, +7, -5, +2$.
• $+ \to -$ (1), $- \to +$ (2), $+ \to -$ (3), $- \to +$ (4) $\implies \mathbf{4}$ sign changes.
Therefore, there are either $4$, $2$, or $0$ positive real roots.

2. Substitute $-x$: $P(-x) = (-x)^4 - 3(-x)^3 + 7(-x)^2 - 5(-x) + 2 = x^4 + 3x^3 + 7x^2 + 5x + 2$.
• All coefficients are positive: $+1, +3, +7, +5, +2 \implies \mathbf{0}$ sign changes.
Therefore, there are zero negative real roots!

7. Exam Answer Blueprint: 10-Mark Model Structure
How to Score Full 10 Marks on "Derive Newton-Raphson & Prove Convergence":
  1. Geometric Diagram (2 Marks): Draw axes, curve $y = f(x)$, root $\alpha$, point $(x_0, f(x_0))$, and tangent line intersecting the $x$-axis at $x_1$.
  2. Taylor Series Derivation (3 Marks): Write $f(x_n + h) = f(x_n) + h f'(x_n) + \dots = 0$. Solve for $h = -f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ to give $x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$.
  3. Quadratic Convergence Proof (3 Marks): Substitute $x_n = \alpha + e_n$, expand $f(\alpha + e_n)$ and $f'(\alpha + e_n)$, use binomial expansion to arrive at $e_{n+1} \approx \frac{f''(\alpha)}{2f'(\alpha)} e_n^2$. Clearly state $p = 2$.
  4. Failure Pitfalls (2 Marks): List $f'(x_n) = 0$ (division by zero), point of inflection, and poor initial guess.
⚠️ Common Exam Pitfalls:
• Forgetting Derivative in Newton-Raphson: Newton-Raphson requires computing $f'(x)$. If the derivative is hard to compute or zero, the method cannot be used.
• Using Radian Mode in Trigonometric Numericals: If solving $f(x) = \cos x - x = 0$, your scientific calculator MUST be set to RADIANS, not DEGREES! Entering degrees results in completely wrong answers!
