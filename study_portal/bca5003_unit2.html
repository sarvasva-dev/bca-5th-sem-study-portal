💡 Conceptual Intuition: How Does Light Stay Inside a Thin Glass Fiber Without Leaking Out?
Imagine a glass swimming pool at night with underwater lights: If light hits the water surface straight up, it exits into the air. But if light hits the surface at a very shallow angle, it doesn't escape—the water surface acts like a perfect shiny mirror and reflects the light right back down into the pool!
This phenomenon is **Total Internal Reflection (TIR)**. In an **Optical Fiber**, we encase an inner glass core (higher refractive index $n_1$) inside an outer glass cladding (lower refractive index $n_2$). Any light entering within the acceptance cone bounces back and forth indefinitely, traveling tens of kilometers at 200,000 km/s with almost zero signal loss!
1. Guided Transmission Media: Twisted Pair, Coaxial & Fiber
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2024–25, 2023–24, 2022–23, 2021 (Compare guided media, why wires are twisted, UTP vs STP)

Guided Media (or bounded / wired media) provide a physical conduit made of copper or glass that directs the electromagnetic signal along a specific physical path.

Why are Copper Wires Twisted in Pairs?
When parallel copper wires run side-by-side, external electromagnetic radiation (noise from motors, fluorescent lights) and adjacent wire signals (**crosstalk**) induce unequal interference in the two wires. By **twisting the wires together**, both wires are equally exposed to the external noise. When the receiver computes the difference between the two wires (Differential Signaling), the identical noise in both wires perfectly cancels out ($V_{signal} = (V_A + V_{noise}) - (V_B + V_{noise}) = V_A - V_B$), preserving data integrity!
2. Optical Fiber & Physics of Total Internal Reflection (TIR)
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2025–26, 2024–25, 2023–24, 2020 (TIR principles, Numerical Aperture, Single-Mode vs Multi-Mode)

Optical fibers operate on the principle of Total Internal Reflection (TIR). Light travels through an inner glass **Core** surrounded by a concentric glass **Cladding**.

Light Propagation in Optical Fiber via Total Internal Reflection
The Two Mandatory Conditions for Total Internal Reflection:
  1. Denser to Rarer Medium: Light must attempt to pass from an optically denser medium into an optically rarer medium. That is, the refractive index of the core ($n_1$) must be strictly greater than that of the cladding ($n_2$): $$n_1 > n_2$$
  2. Angle of Incidence Exceeds Critical Angle: The angle of incidence $\theta$ at the core-cladding boundary must be greater than the **Critical Angle** $\theta_c$: $$\theta > \theta_c \quad \text{where} \quad \theta_c = \arcsin\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)$$

Acceptance Angle and Numerical Aperture ($NA$):

Light rays can only undergo TIR if they enter the fiber face within a conical angle called the **Acceptance Cone**. The sine of the maximum half-angle of this cone is called the **Numerical Aperture ($NA$)**, which measures the light-gathering capability of the fiber:

$$NA = \sin(\theta_{max}) = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2}$$ $$\text{Acceptance Angle } \alpha = \arcsin(NA) = \arcsin\left(\sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2}\right)$$

Single-Mode vs. Multi-Mode Optical Fiber:

Factor Single-Mode Fiber (SMF) Multi-Mode Fiber (MMF)
Core Diameter Extremely narrow: 8 to 10 $\mu m$ (approaching light wavelength). Much wider: 50 to 62.5 $\mu m$.
Light Propagation Only ONE single light ray path (zero modal dispersion). Multiple rays travel at different reflection angles simultaneously.
Light Source Expensive, highly focused Semiconductor Laser Diode. Inexpensive Light Emitting Diode (LED).
Modal Dispersion Zero modal dispersion; signal retains pristine shape over vast spans. High modal dispersion (different rays arrive at slightly different times, smearing the pulse).
Typical Distance Long-haul telecommunications (10 km to 100+ km). Short distances: LAN backbones, campus buildings (up to 2 km).
3. Unguided Wireless Media & Satellite Orbits
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2023–24, 2021, 2019 (Radio vs Microwave vs Infrared, Satellite communication orbits)

Unguided Media (wireless communication) transmit electromagnetic waves through the air, vacuum, or water without using a physical conductor.

Wireless Wave Frequency Range Propagation Characteristics Applications
Radio Waves $3\text{ kHz}$ to $1\text{ GHz}$ Omnidirectional (sends in all directions); easily penetrates physical walls; travels long distances. FM/AM Radio, television broadcasting, cellular networks.
Microwaves $1\text{ GHz}$ to $300\text{ GHz}$ Unidirectional (Line-of-Sight); requires narrowly focused parabolic dish antennas; cannot penetrate buildings; rain attenuation. Point-to-point microwave towers, satellite communications, Wi-Fi, radar.
Infrared $300\text{ GHz}$ to $400\text{ THz}$ Very short range; strictly line-of-sight; cannot penetrate solid walls (provides inherent privacy). TV remote controls, wireless computer mice, medical sensors.

Satellite Communication Orbits:

  • GEO (Geostationary Earth Orbit): Located at exactly $35,786\text{ km}$ directly above the equator. The satellite orbits at the exact same rotational speed as the Earth (orbital period = 24 hours), appearing completely stationary in the sky. Three GEO satellites spaced $120^\circ$ apart cover the entire globe! Used for satellite TV, weather monitoring. Round-trip propagation latency is $\sim 500\text{ ms}$.
  • MEO (Medium Earth Orbit): Located at $5,000$ to $20,000\text{ km}$ (e.g. GPS constellation at $20,200\text{ km}$, 12-hour orbital period).
  • LEO (Low Earth Orbit): Located at $500$ to $1,500\text{ km}$ (e.g. Starlink, Iridium). Low latency ($\sim 20\text{ ms}$), but satellites orbit rapidly ($\sim 90\text{ minutes}$), requiring constellations of thousands of satellites.
4. Theoretical Channel Capacity: Nyquist vs. Shannon
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2024–25, 2023–24, 2022–23, 2021 (Calculate channel capacity using Nyquist and Shannon formulas)

Data transmission speed is constrained by physics and noise. Two foundational theorems govern theoretical channel maximums:

1. Nyquist Bit Rate Formula (For Noiseless Channel):
Formulated by Harry Nyquist (1928), gives the maximum theoretical bit rate of a noiseless channel of bandwidth $B$ with $M$ discrete signal levels: $$\mathbf{C_{Nyquist} = 2B \log_2 M} \quad \text{(bits per second)}$$
2. Shannon Capacity Formula (For Real Noisy Channel):
Formulated by Claude Shannon (1948), gives the absolute theoretical upper bound on capacity in the presence of thermal white noise: $$\mathbf{C_{Shannon} = B \log_2(1 + SNR)} \quad \text{(bits per second)}$$ where $B$ is bandwidth in Hertz, and $SNR$ is the linear Signal-to-Noise Ratio: $$SNR_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}(SNR) \implies SNR = 10^{(SNR_{dB} / 10)}$$

Step-by-Step Solved University Numerical Problem

Problem (CSJMU 2023–24): A standard telephone channel has a bandwidth of $B = 3000\text{ Hz}$ and a Signal-to-Noise ratio of $SNR_{dB} = 30\text{ dB}$. Calculate:

  1. The maximum Shannon channel capacity.
  2. How many discrete signal levels ($M$) are required to achieve this bit rate according to Nyquist's formula?

Step 1: Convert $SNR_{dB}$ to linear $SNR$:

$$30 = 10 \log_{10}(SNR) \implies \log_{10}(SNR) = 3 \implies SNR = 10^3 = \mathbf{1000}$$

Step 2: Calculate Shannon Capacity:

$$C = B \log_2(1 + SNR) = 3000 \cdot \log_2(1 + 1000) = 3000 \cdot \log_2(1001)$$

Since $2^9 = 512$ and $2^{10} = 1024$, $\log_2(1001) \approx 9.967$:

$$C = 3000 \times 9.967 = \mathbf{29,901 \text{ bps}} \approx \mathbf{30 \text{ kbps}}$$

Step 3: Equate to Nyquist's formula to find signal levels $M$:

$$C = 2B \log_2 M \implies 29901 = 2 \times 3000 \times \log_2 M = 6000 \log_2 M$$ $$\log_2 M = \frac{29901}{6000} \approx 4.98 \approx 5 \implies M = 2^5 = \mathbf{32 \text{ discrete signal levels}}$$
5. Exam Answer Blueprint: 10-Mark Model Structure
How to Score Full 10 Marks on "Optical Fiber Transmission & TIR":
  1. Definition (1 Mark): State that optical fiber uses silica glass to transmit data as modulated light pulses.
  2. Structural Diagram (3 Marks): Draw a neat cross-section showing Core ($n_1$), Cladding ($n_2$), and Buffer Coating. Draw light bouncing inside core.
  3. Two Mandatory TIR Conditions (2 Marks): State $n_1 > n_2$ and angle of incidence $\theta > \theta_c$.
  4. Mathematical Formulas (2 Marks): Write the critical angle formula $\theta_c = \arcsin(n_2/n_1)$ and Numerical Aperture $NA = \sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2}$.
  5. Single-Mode vs Multi-Mode (2 Marks): Tabulate core diameter ($8\mu m$ vs $50\mu m$), light source (Laser vs LED), and distance capacity.
⚠️ Common Exam Pitfalls:
• Confusing dB with Linear SNR: Never plug $SNR_{dB}$ directly into the Shannon formula! $30\text{ dB}$ must be converted to $SNR = 10^{30/10} = 1000$ before calculating $\log_2(1+SNR)$.
• Inverting Refractive Indices: The core index $n_1$ MUST be greater than cladding index $n_2$. If you write $n_2 > n_1$, TIR is physically impossible and evaluators award 0 marks!
