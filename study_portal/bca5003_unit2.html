1. Guided Media: Twisted Pair, Coaxial & Optical Fiber
🎯 Learning Objectives:
  • Compare UTP, STP, Coaxial cable, and Optical Fiber across bandwidth, attenuation, and cost.
  • Understand why twisting wires cancels out electromagnetic interference (crosstalk).
  • Explain Total Internal Reflection (TIR) in Optical Fiber: $n_1 > n_2$ and $\theta > \theta_c$.
Medium Physical Construction Bandwidth & Attenuation Susceptibility to EMI Noise
Twisted Pair (UTP / STP) Two insulated copper wires twisted spirally to cancel crosstalk; RJ-45 connector Moderate (10 Mbps to 10 Gbps; up to 100 meters per run) Moderate; STP includes braided foil shield against external EMI
Coaxial Cable Central solid copper conductor surrounded by dielectric insulation, metallic braid shield, and plastic jacket High (up to 500 MHz; used in cable TV and early Ethernet 10Base2/10Base5) Low; metal shielding blocks most industrial interference
Optical Fiber Glass/plastic Core ($n_1$) surrounded by reflective Cladding ($n_2$) with protective coating Extremely High (Terabits/sec); negligible attenuation over 50–100 km Zero (Immune to electromagnetic interference, lightning, and radio noise)
Total Internal Reflection in Optical Fiber: Light bounces losslessly along the core.
Conditions for Total Internal Reflection (TIR):
  1. The light ray must travel from an optically denser medium to an optically rarer medium: Core index $n_1 >$ Cladding index $n_2$.
  2. The angle of incidence at the core-cladding boundary must exceed the Critical Angle $\theta_c$, given by: $$\sin \theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1} \implies \theta_c = \arcsin\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)$$
2. Channel Capacity: Nyquist vs Shannon Capacity
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2024–25, 2021, 2018 (Theorems, differences, and numerical calculations)
⚡ Essential Pass Content
1. Nyquist Bit Rate (For Ideal Noiseless Channel):
$$C = 2B \log_2(M) \quad \text{bps}$$ where $B$ is bandwidth in Hertz, and $M$ is the number of discrete signal levels.
2. Shannon Capacity Theorem (For Real Noisy Channel):
$$C = B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}) \quad \text{bps}$$ where $B$ is bandwidth in Hertz, and $\text{SNR}$ is the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (linear, not in dB!).
Note on Decibels: $\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}(\text{SNR}) \implies \text{SNR} = 10^{(\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} / 10)}$.
📝 Solved University Numerical:
A telephone line has a bandwidth of $4 \text{ kHz}$ and a signal-to-noise ratio ($\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}}$) of $30 \text{ dB}$. Calculate the maximum theoretical channel capacity.
Step 1: Convert SNR from dB to Linear Ratio:
$$30 = 10 \log_{10}(\text{SNR}) \implies \log_{10}(\text{SNR}) = 3 \implies \text{SNR} = 10^3 = \mathbf{1000}$$
Step 2: Apply Shannon's Formula:
Bandwidth $B = 4 \text{ kHz} = 4000 \text{ Hz}$.
$$C = B \log_2(1 + \text{SNR}) = 4000 \log_2(1 + 1000) = 4000 \log_2(1001)$$
Step 3: Convert Base-2 Log to Base-10:
$$\log_2(1001) = \frac{\log_{10}(1001)}{\log_{10}(2)} \approx \frac{3.00043}{0.30103} \approx \mathbf{9.967}$$ $$C = 4000 \times 9.967 = \mathbf{39,869 \text{ bps}} \approx \mathbf{39.87 \text{ kbps}}$$
3. Transmission Impairments in Physical Channels
⭐ CSJMU PYQs: 2023–24, 2019 (Explain Attenuation, Distortion, and Noise)
The 3 Causes of Signal Degradation:
  1. Attenuation: Loss of energy as a signal travels over distance through a medium. Compensated using amplifiers (analog) or repeaters (digital).
  2. Distortion: The signal changes shape because composite signals contain multiple harmonic frequencies, and each frequency component travels at a slightly different speed (propagation delay).
  3. Noise: Unwanted external energy added to the signal:
    • Thermal Noise: Random movement of electrons in wires (cannot be eliminated).
    • Crosstalk: Interference caused by magnetic fields of adjacent parallel wire pairs.
    • Impulse Noise: Sudden high-energy spikes caused by lightning, power surges, or industrial machinery.
