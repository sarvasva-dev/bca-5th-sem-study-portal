1. Concept of Knowledge Management & DIKW Hierarchy
📌 Syllabus Core Focus: Definition of KM, DIKW Pyramid, and Enterprise Knowledge Life Cycle

Knowledge Management (KM) is the systematic, cross-functional process of creating, acquiring, structuring, sharing, and utilizing intellectual assets to enhance organizational performance, decision-making, and competitive advantage.

The Classic DIKW (Data → Information → Knowledge → Wisdom) Hierarchy
The 4 DIKW Levels Compared:
  • Data: Unprocessed, raw objective facts and symbols without context (e.g., 100, 120, 150).
  • Information: Data given structure, context, and purpose (e.g., "Monthly sales grew from 100 to 150 units in Q3").
  • Knowledge: Information blended with human experience, rules, contextual understanding, and intuition (e.g., "Demand surges during the Diwali festive season, requiring higher safety stock").
  • Wisdom: The ability to apply knowledge prudently for ethical, long-term strategic decisions (e.g., "Balancing inventory buildup against holding costs, credit risks, and supplier lead times").

3. Structure & Architecture of a KMS
📌 Exam Blueprint: The Three Pillars: People, Processes, and Technology

A successful KMS is never merely software. It represents a socio-technical equilibrium supported by three intertwined pillars:

The 3 Pillars of KMS:
  1. People & Culture: Knowledge champions, subject matter experts (SMEs), and general employees. Crucially requires a trust-based culture where knowledge sharing is recognized and rewarded rather than hoarded.
  2. Processes: Formal workflows for knowledge harvesting, peer review, codification, validation, indexing, and regular retirement of outdated material.
  3. Technology: The digital infrastructure providing secure repositories, semantic search engines, collaborative workspaces, and AI-driven recommendation feeds.
Architectural Layer Components & Technologies
User Interface Layer Enterprise Portals, Mobile Dashboards, Collaborative Chatbots, Discussion Wikis
Knowledge Services Layer Semantic Search Engines, Rule-Based Taxonomies, Document Versioning, Workflow Routing
Repository Layer Document Management Systems (DMS), Best-Practice Libraries, FAQ Archives, Relational Databases
Governance & Security Layer Role-Based Access Control (RBAC), Intellectual Property (IP) Auditing, Compliance Tracking
4. Techniques & Strategies of Knowledge Management
⭐ High-Yield Concept: Codification vs Personalization Strategy (Hansen, Nohria & Tierney)

Organizations balance two primary strategic orientations:

Dimension Codification Strategy ("People-to-Documents") Personalization Strategy ("Person-to-Person")
Core Model Knowledge is codified and stored in databases where it can be retrieved and reused. Knowledge is tied to the person who developed it and is shared through direct dialogue.
Focus High reuse of standardized explicit knowledge. Customized advice for unique, complex, unstructured problems.
IT Investment Heavy investment in search engines, document repositories, and templates. Investment in networks connecting experts (Yellow Pages / Expert Directories).
Industry Example Dell, large IT software support, routine tax audit firms. Bain, McKinsey, specialized surgery teams, boutique R&D labs.
High-Yield KM Capture & Sharing Techniques:
  • Communities of Practice (CoPs): Voluntary groups of practitioners who share a common passion or role, collaborating informally to solve mutual challenges.
  • After-Action Reviews (AARs): Structured debriefings held immediately after a project or critical incident to document: What happened? Why did it happen? What will we do differently next time?
  • Knowledge Storytelling: Preserves the tacit nuance, human emotions, and decision contexts of past crises that cannot be captured in sterile numeric tables.
  • Mentoring & Job Shadowing: Pairing apprentices with retiring master craftsmen to transfer tacit muscle memory and diagnostic heuristics.
5. Appreciation (Benefits) & Limitations of KMS
📌 Exam Essay Question: Organizational Value Creation vs Real-world Implementation Barriers
Appreciation: Key Organizational Benefits:
  1. Prevents "Reinventing the Wheel": Reusing pre-tested software modules or engineering designs slashes project delivery times.
  2. Mitigates Brain Drain: When senior personnel resign or retire, their codified experience remains embedded within organizational assets.
  3. Speeds Up Onboarding: New hires reach peak productivity in days instead of months through structured knowledge repositories.
  4. Fosters Accelerated Innovation: Cross-departmental knowledge sharing sparks novel solutions by connecting disparate ideas.
Critical Limitations & Implementation Pitfalls:
  • The "Knowledge is Power" Cultural Barrier: Employees fear that sharing their exclusive expertise renders them replaceable.
  • Information Overload & Outdated Knowledge: Repositories become digital graveyards if unmanaged, storing contradictory or obsolete data.
  • High Measurement Difficulty: The Return on Investment (ROI) of KM is difficult to isolate on a quarterly balance sheet.
  • Over-Reliance on Technology Alone: Buying an expensive portal software without addressing corporate culture leads to guaranteed failure.
